# 00. Running AI Security at a Company

## What this part is

The [main course](https://github.com/rajumandala/aisec-notes/blob/main/00.%20Syllabus.md) secured one AI order assistant inside a broker's trading app, with its controls in code. [Part S](https://github.com/rajumandala/adlc/blob/main/notes/S0.%20AI%20in%20the%20SDLC.md) looked at the team that builds it with AI, and [Part M](https://github.com/rajumandala/ai-supplychain/blob/main/notes/M0.%20Model%20Supply%20Chain.md) at the models it depends on. All three looked at one system.

This part looks at the whole company. The broker now has many AI features from many teams, and a small security team. The questions change from "is this assistant safe?" to:

- Which controls should every team get for free, and which must each team build for its own business?
- Which features need a deep review, and which need none?
- How does the company know it is getting safer, and what does it report?
- What has gone wrong at other companies, and what would have stopped it?
- Why can't the model provider just fix prompt injection?
- What do regulators, auditors and clients ask for, and how is it answered once?
- What changes when attackers use AI against the company?

The example stays the same broker, with the same people and the same controls. Where a control was built in the main course, the notes link to it.

## Scope and adoption

01–07 are the reference architecture and threat foundations. 08–14 add the operating model for a multinational: authority, lifecycle gates, regional obligations, suppliers, response, funding, assurance and reusable records. The broker examples now include multiple entities and countries; they are not a claim that one country's rules apply everywhere.

The scope includes company-built AI, purchased/SaaS AI, employee assistants, agents, local/self-hosted models and vendor-enabled features. Existing security, privacy, legal, procurement, model-risk, Responsible AI and audit functions retain their responsibilities. AI security coordinates its part; it does not become the owner of every AI risk.

All controls and templates here are **proposals for company adoption**, not approved corporate policy. “Must” describes a requirement in the proposed standard. An accountable executive must approve the scope, policy hierarchy, authority limits, regional overlays and funding before enforcement. Examples, staffing assumptions and service targets are illustrative. Implementation, operating effectiveness, certification and legal compliance are shown only by actual evidence and independent review. Legal owns current applicability and effective dates, not this course.

Use three layers: these explanatory notes; adopted mandatory standards by tier; and operational workflows with evidence in the company's controlled system of record. Start with the charter and inventory, pilot the gates on a few real systems, test containment and recovery, and only then scale.

## How the notes are built

The notes follow the same pattern as the earlier parts:

1. Each note starts from the problem the previous one ended on.
2. It adds one company-wide practice: a shared service, a process or a measure.
3. It shows the point with an example from the broker. Most of this part is design and process, with little code.
4. It lists the OWASP risks it meets, each with an example, the issue and the fix.
5. It ends on the next problem and links to the note that deals with it. The operating guides add decision tables and evidence checklists; mappings illustrate security coverage, not completeness of governance or law.

## The concepts

| # | Concept | What the company gains | Security point | OWASP | Ends on this problem |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 01 | [The AI security platform](01.%20The%20AI%20Security%20Platform.md) | Shared services for every AI feature: an AI gateway, guardrails, an MCP registry, agent identity, retrieval, sandboxes and evals | The platform owns who is calling, which model, what leaves and what is recorded; the app owns what each action may do. Managed API calls use enforced gateway routes; SaaS/local AI need equivalent reviewed controls and inventory reconciliation. The gateway is now the most sensitive system, and it sees text, not meaning. Failover to a backup model is a model change: an evaluated snapshot, or fail closed | LLM02, LLM06, ASI04, ASI03 | Features are not equally dangerous, and the security team has time for only a few deep reviews |
| 02 | [Intake and risk tiers](02.%20Intake%20and%20Risk%20Tiers.md) | A short intake for every AI feature, including vendor AI, and a tier computed from facts: what it can do, what data it touches, what untrusted text it reads, whether anything can leave, who uses it | The tier comes from platform registries, not the team's opinion. The lethal trifecta or acting alone is tier 3 on its own. The cheapest way down a tier is a design change. The tier is recomputed on every new scope, tool, source or way out, and a higher tier blocks the deploy. Unregistered gateway keys, blocked provider calls, staff consent logs and vendor settings find the rest. An assistant for every employee makes old over-sharing easy to find, so permissions are fixed before it is switched on, and pages many people can edit are untrusted text for everyone who reads them. The business owner signs the remaining risk, and the acceptance expires | LLM03, LLM02, LLM04, ASI03 | The company cannot yet show that it is safer than last year |
| 03 | [Metrics and roadmap](03.%20Metrics%20and%20Roadmap.md) | Measures for coverage, effectiveness and exposure, a one-page board report, and a roadmap built from the gaps | Counting reviews and blocked prompts says nothing. Coverage comes from the registries and its target is 100%. Attack success rate on a fixed suite gets tuned to, so add a fresh suite and count attacks stopped by code, not by the model. Canaries and drills measure time to detect and time to stop. The red team works to written rules, tests production with test accounts, and uses tools for regressions and people for new attacks. Since injection is unsolved, measure the worst case if the model is fully controlled, and fix features where it is unbounded. Most of the work is owned outside the AI security team; a small committee decides only what the tiers cannot, and the first 90 days start from an inventory built from bills and DNS logs | LLM01, LLM03, ASI10, LLM06 | The team writes its own tests, and the broker has had no serious incident to learn from |
| 04 | [Incident case studies](04.%20Incident%20Case%20Studies.md) | Eleven public AI incidents (Chevrolet, Air Canada, Slack AI, GitHub MCP, EchoLeak, ForcedLeak, Replit, Amazon Q, Salesloft Drift, Samsung, ChatGPT), each mapped to a pattern, a control and a test, and an incident response plan for AI | Every case where the model was fooled also had a way out, a broad token or an unchecked answer. Several needed no model at all. The soft controls failed, and a hard control failed when nobody maintained it: an allowlisted domain expired. Each case ends as an eval case, and poisoned memory, indexes and summaries must be cleaned after an incident. Outside reports are rated by what the attacker reached, not by how bad the text looks | LLM01, LLM07, LLM03, ASI04 | Vendors patch the way out, never the model following the attacker's text |
| 05 | [Why prompt injection is unsolved](05.%20Why%20Prompt%20Injection%20Is%20Unsolved.md) | An answer to "why can't the provider fix it?", the research defences at four levels, and how to judge a defence's numbers | SQL injection was solved by a separate channel; a model has one token stream, and roles are trained habits. Reading instructions in data is part of the job, intent is not in the text, and the attacker moves second. Spotlighting, instruction hierarchy, StruQ and SecAlign, and classifiers lower the rate; adaptive attacks broke most published defences. Only system-level designs such as CaMeL give guarantees, at a cost in usefulness. Model gains never move a feature down a tier | LLM01, ASI01, LLM03 | Regulators, auditors and customers ask in their own frameworks |
| 06 | [Standards map](06.%20Standards%20Map.md) | One control list with owners and evidence, mapped to NIST AI RMF and its Generative AI Profile, ISO/IEC 42001, the EU AI Act and MITRE ATLAS | Each framework does a different job: guidance, a certifiable management system, law, an attack knowledge base. Map from the controls outwards, and take evidence from the platform once. Legal owns a source-backed obligations register; reported amendments are not assumed to be law. Compliance is not security: a mapping or certificate does not prove a feature is safe or lawful, and a certificate covers a management system, not a model | LLM01, LLM02, ASI04 | Attackers use AI against processes that have no AI in them |
| 07 | [AI-enabled attacks on the company](07.%20AI-Enabled%20Attacks%20on%20the%20Company.md) | Deepfake calls, cloned voices, generated identity documents, AI-written phishing and attackers' agents, from five public cases, mapped to the broker's processes | AI makes old attacks cheaper. Detecting fakes is a classifier against an attacker who moves second, so trust moves to what AI cannot fake: a passkey, a call back on a channel the requester did not choose, two approvers, low limits for new accounts. Train on process, not on spotting fakes. The company's own AI tools can be used by malware | ASI02; ATLAS AML.T0088, AML.T0073, AML.T0052 | Who has authority across entities and countries? |
