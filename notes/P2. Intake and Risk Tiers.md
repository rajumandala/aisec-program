# P2. Intake and Risk Tiers

P1 ended with a list of thirty AI features and a security team that can do only a few deep reviews a quarter. A research summary and a mandate that sells shares cannot get the same review. Someone has to decide which feature gets which controls, and the decision cannot depend on who shouts loudest or who remembered to ask.

## The idea

Sort every AI feature into a **risk tier** from a few facts about it, and let the tier decide the controls and the review. The facts are the ones the main course kept coming back to:

- **What can it do?** Answer only, propose an action, act after a person confirms, or act on its own (notes 4, 12a).
- **What data does it touch?** Public, internal, client data, or regulated data such as PAN and bank details (notes 7a, 10a).
- **What untrusted content does it read?** News, uploads, emails, tickets, web pages, other agents (notes 7, 9a, 13).
- **Can anything leave?** Links, images, emails, web requests, replies to someone else, calls to outside agents (notes 10a, 10b).
- **Who uses it?** Staff, clients, or anyone on the internet.

Two rules make this work:

- **The tier comes from facts, not opinions.** "Is this high risk?" gets a different answer from every team. "Can it place an order?" does not.
- **The facts come from the platform where possible.** A team's own answers are a starting point. The scopes in the agent identity service, the servers in the MCP registry and the document labels in the retrieval service (P1) say what the feature can really do.

In AppSec terms, this is data classification and application risk rating, applied to AI features. What is new is that "what can it do" and "what untrusted text does it read" matter more than what language it is written in.

This note is design and process, not an implemented intake service. The illustrative function has been locally checked against nine synthetic classification cases; no production integrations, controls or operating workflows have been tested.

## Step 1: the intake

Every new AI feature, and every AI feature a vendor switches on in a tool the company already uses, starts with an intake. Keep it short: a long form gets copied from the last one.

| Question | Answer options | Checked against |
|---|---|---|
| What can it do? | answers / proposes / acts after confirmation / acts alone | Scopes registered in the agent identity service |
| Can it move money, place orders or change client records? | yes / no | Scopes |
| Most sensitive data it reads | public / internal / client / regulated | Retrieval service labels; tools it can call |
| Does it read text that someone outside the company can write? | yes / no, with a list | MCP registry; retrieval sources; upload endpoints |
| Can anything it writes leave the company or reach another person? | yes / no, with a list | Rendering rules; tools such as email or HTTP; replies to other users |
| Who uses it? | staff / clients / public | Gateway app key; where it is deployed |
| Model and provider | from the approved list | Gateway |
| Business owner | a named person | Note 15's "Who owns what" table |

Priya, a developer from Part S, fills one in for a tool that drafts release notes from pull requests. Most answers take her a minute, because the platform already knows them: the app key, the model, the scopes. The questions she has to think about are the untrusted text (pull request descriptions from outside contributors) and the way out (the notes are published on the website).

The last row matters most. Every feature has a person in the business who owns it and who can accept its remaining risk. A feature with no owner does not ship.

The intake also records stable system and parent/child IDs, technical owner, countries/entities, intended/excluded uses, population and scale, critical dependencies, model/data/tool versions, data-flow diagram and linked legal/supplier reviews. Use [P14](P14.%20Operating%20Templates%20and%20Worked%20Example.md) for the reusable record.

## Step 2: the tier

The tier is a function of the facts. Writing it as code makes the rules exact and lets the platform compute it, not a person:

```python
def tier(f) -> int:
    if f.consequential_use or f.safety_impact or f.critical_service:
        return 3
    trifecta = f.data != "public" and f.reads_untrusted and f.can_send_out
    if f.acts == "alone" or trifecta:
        return 3
    if f.acts == "after_confirmation" and f.moves_money:
        return 3
    if f.acts != "answers" or f.data in {"client", "regulated"}:
        return 2
    if f.users != "staff" or f.reads_untrusted:
        return 1
    return 0
```

This illustrative function is a **security screening floor**, not a complete risk score. Missing facts block classification; they do not default to `False`. Consequential use includes material recommendations about employment, credit, insurance, healthcare or access to services, even when a human decides. A KYC tool that only extracts fields differs from one recommending rejection; the latter is tier 3.

Also record impact magnitude, population and vulnerable groups, reversibility, countries, languages, business criticality, scale, concentration and fraud/availability exposure. A potentially severe impact requires tier 3 review. Keep inherent risk, tested controls and residual risk separate. Legal/privacy record regulatory classification and organizational role independently: a low security tier cannot waive a legal duty. Human confirmation counts only when a competent reviewer sees the exact action and supporting evidence, has time and authority to reject it, and is not rubber-stamping.

Three further things in it are deliberate:

- **The lethal trifecta from note 10b is tier 3 on its own,** even for a feature that only answers. Private data means anything that is not public, internal included. The Slack AI case in note 7a was a search feature that answered questions, and what it leaked through a link was a message from a private channel: internal data, not client data.
- **Acting alone is tier 3,** whatever the data. Note 12a showed that removing the person moves all the approval to setup time, and that needs the deepest review.
- **The cheapest way down a tier is a design change, not an argument.** A support-drafting tool with the trifecta drops to tier 2 only if tested recipient and output controls remove all attacker-usable egress. Removing links/images alone does not stop plain-text leaks or tool-based exfiltration. That gives teams a reason to remove a leg of the trifecta, which is the outcome the security team wanted anyway.

## Step 3: what each tier gets

| Tier | Example at the broker | Controls | Review |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | A staff tool that summarises public exchange circulars | The P1 template: gateway, tracing, standard eval cases | No bespoke security review; baseline evidence, owner approval and legal screening still required |
| 1 | Research summaries of public news for clients | Tier 0, plus the 10b checklist answered in the pull request, guardrails on output (note 10a) | Automated checks. Security reads one in five checklists |
| 2 | KYC document reader (regulated data, reads uploads, no way out) | Tier 1, plus the team's own security eval cases, permission-aware retrieval (note 7a), data minimised before the model call (note 14) | A design review of about two hours, done by a security engineer or a trained champion in the team |
| 3 | The order assistant; the morning watcher; support drafts with links | Tier 2, plus a threat model (note 14), a red-team exercise before launch (note 10), monitoring signals and a tested kill switch (notes 12a, 14), risk acceptance signed by the business owner (note 15) | A deep review by the security team, and again on any change to tools, data, autonomy or model |

Two warnings about the table:

- **Tier 0 is not "safe".** It means the platform's defaults are enough for what it can do today. If it gains a tool, it is a new feature.
- **The review checks the design, not the tier.** A reviewer who finds the trifecta in a tier 2 feature has found a wrong fact in the intake, and the fact gets fixed in the registry, not only in the report.

### The capacity check

Tiers are only useful if they match the team's time. With the broker's thirty features, the split might be:

| Tier | Features | Review time each | Total per quarter |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 9 | 0 | 0 |
| 1 | 10 | about 30 minutes for the sampled ones | 1 hour |
| 2 | 7 | 2 hours | 14 hours |
| 3 | 4 | 3 to 5 days, plus re-reviews | 15 to 25 days |

If tier 3 does not fit, the answer is not to shrink the review. It is to move features down by design (remove a leg of the trifecta, add a confirmation, drop a scope), or to train champions in the product teams to do the tier 2 reviews. Count tier 3 features over time: P3 uses that number.

## Step 4: tiers change, so recompute them

A tier set once at launch goes stale. The order assistant started in note 1 as a model call that returned JSON. By note 12a it could sell shares while the investor slept. Each step was small, and none of them would have triggered a new review on its own.

So the platform recomputes the tier whenever a fact changes:

- **A new scope** requested from the agent identity service, such as `orders:place` or a mandate grant.
- **A new MCP server or tool** added in the registry.
- **A new data source** in the retrieval service, or a new label on an existing one.
- **A new way out:** links allowed in replies, an email tool, a call to an outside agent.
- **A new model**, provider, prompt/retrieval behavior, intended use, population, country, scale or critical dependency; material vendor changes count too.

If the new tier is higher, the change does not deploy until the review for that tier is done. This is the same idea as note 6, where the assistant refused to start when a server's tools changed: the reviewed version is pinned, and drift stops the line.

A model change alone keeps the tier but re-runs the evals. A model that follows injected text more often makes every tier 3 feature riskier, and only the eval results show it (note 14a).

## Step 5: the features nobody brought to intake

Intake only covers features that teams bring to it. Four places show the rest:

- **The gateway.** Map each active app key to a stable system ID; keys and systems are not one-to-one. A key with traffic and no intake record is a finding.
- **Blocked provider calls.** P1 blocks direct calls to model providers. The block log shows which hosts tried, which is a list of features built around the gateway.
- **Staff consent.** Staff connect AI apps and agents to their own work email, calendar and files, by clicking "allow" on a consent screen. That traffic never reaches the gateway. The identity provider's log of consents and connected apps does show it. Set the identity provider so that apps asking for broad access to mail or files need an administrator's approval, and treat each approved one as vendor AI.
- **Vendor AI.** A support desk, CRM or document tool the company already pays for switches on an AI feature that reads tickets and emails and writes replies. It is the same as building it in-house: the same intake questions, plus the vendor's terms for storage, training and sub-processors (note 14). These are often the hardest tier 3 cases, because the company cannot change the design. The options are a setting, a contract term, or not switching it on.

Staff pasting client data into a public chatbot is the same problem from the other side. The answer is an approved tool through the gateway that is easier than the public one, plus data-loss rules on the network for the rest. Blocking without an approved tool sends people to their phones.

## Step 6: switching on an assistant for every employee

The biggest vendor AI decision is usually an assistant for every employee, built into the email, documents and chat tools the company already uses. It searches everything the employee can open and answers in plain English. That is note 7a's permission-aware retrieval, done by the vendor, and it is done correctly: it shows each person only what they already have access to.

The problem is that access. Most companies have years of files shared with "everyone", folders left open after a project, and links that anyone in the company can follow. Nobody found them, because nobody knew where to look. An assistant that searches everything finds them in one question: "what are the salary bands for my team?", "show me the board papers on the acquisition", "which clients complained about the CEO?". Nothing is broken and nobody is attacked. The assistant only made existing over-sharing easy to find.

So the work before switching it on is mostly not AI work:

- **Find the over-shared places first.** Sites and folders open to everyone, sharing links that work for the whole company, files with sensitive labels in open places. The vendors that sell these assistants also sell reports for this, and Microsoft publishes a rollout guide for its assistant built around it (Sources).
- **Fix permissions, or hide what cannot be fixed yet.** Some tools can exclude chosen sites from the assistant's search while their permissions are cleaned up. That is a temporary measure with an owner and a date, like an exception in P1.
- **Label sensitive data,** so the assistant and the data-loss rules can treat it differently. Labels are also what note 7a's derived-data rule needs: a summary inherits the strictest label of what it was built from.
- **Pilot with a small group,** watch what they find, fix that, then widen.
- **Count who can write what it reads.** Email from outside is the obvious untrusted text. So are documents shared from outside the company, calendar invites, chat channels that guests can join, and wiki pages that hundreds of staff and contractors can edit. One planted instruction in a page many people read reaches every employee whose question retrieves it, and stays until someone finds it. That is note 7's poisoned post inside the company. Pages that many people can edit and many people read get an owner and a change log, and the assistant shows which documents an answer came from, so a strange answer can be traced.
- **Run the intake anyway.** The assistant reads untrusted text and can show links and images, which is a way out. EchoLeak in P4 was exactly this kind of assistant. It is tier 3 by the rule in step 2, whatever the vendor's certificates say.

The data owner of each system decides who should see it, not the security team. The assistant rollout is often the first time anyone has asked them.

## Step 7: risk acceptance

Every tier 3 review ends with a list of what is still open. Note 14 had one: the price band fails open when the exchange feed is down. Someone decides whether that is acceptable, and it is not the security team:

- **Security writes the risk** in business terms: what can go wrong, how likely, what it would cost, and what would fix it.
- **The business owner funds the fix or requests acceptance within P8's delegated authority,** with required security/risk concurrence. Nobody may accept unlawful processing or waive a statutory prohibition. Note 15's "Who owns what" table names that person for the assistant.
- **The acceptance expires,** usually after six or twelve months, or at the next tier 3 change. Then it comes back.

An accepted risk is not a failure of the review. A risk nobody wrote down is.

## OWASP risks in this note

The codes come from the [OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications](https://github.com/rajumandala/aisec-notes/blob/main/A1.%20OWASP%20Top%2010%20for%20LLM%20Applications.md) and the [OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications](https://github.com/rajumandala/aisec-notes/blob/main/A2.%20OWASP%20Top%2010%20for%20Agentic%20Applications.md), both 2026 editions. The operational controls have not been run, so each example shows what would happen.

### LLM03: Excessive Agency

- **Example:** The research summary feature is reviewed as tier 1. Six months later a developer adds `orders:place` so it can offer a "buy" button, and nobody reviews it again.
- **Issue:** The agency grew one small step at a time, and the review was for a feature that no longer exists.
- **Fix:** The tier is recomputed from the agent identity service on every new scope, and a higher tier blocks the deploy until it is reviewed.

### LLM02: Sensitive Information Disclosure

- **Example:** The support desk vendor switches on AI reply drafts. They read every ticket, including bank statements clients attached, and the vendor keeps the text for training.
- **Issue:** A tier 3 feature arrived through a settings page and never came to intake.
- **Fix:** Vendor AI features go through the same intake, with the vendor's storage and training terms. Until then the setting stays off.

### LLM04: Supply Chain

- **Example:** A team builds a feature on a provider outside the approved list, with a personal key, and the gateway never sees it.
- **Issue:** A model provider the company never assessed, holding client data, with no inventory record.
- **Fix:** Direct provider calls are blocked (P1). The block log and the gateway's key list are checked against intake records every week.

### ASI03: Identity and Privilege Abuse

- **Example:** The team behind the KYC reader writes "internal data only" on the intake. Its service identity can read every client's documents.
- **Issue:** The tier came from the team's answer, not from what the identity can reach.
- **Fix:** Intake answers are checked against the agent identity service and retrieval labels. A mismatch is a finding, and the higher answer wins.
