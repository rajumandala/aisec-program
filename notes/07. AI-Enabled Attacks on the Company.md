# 07. AI-Enabled Attacks on the Company

06 ended with the company's AI features mapped to every framework that asks about them. All of Part P so far has been about the AI the company builds or buys. Attackers use AI too, and most of what they attack has no AI in it: the account opening process, the call centre, the payments desk, the staff inbox and the developers' laptops.

## The idea

Many security checks rest on signals that used to be hard to fake: a face on a video call, a voice on the phone, a photo of an identity document, a well-written email from a colleague, the time it takes to find and exploit a weak server. Generative AI makes each of these cheaper to fake or faster to do. The attacks are not new. Their cost has fallen.

So the answer is not mainly to detect fakes. Detection is a classifier against an attacker who moves second, the same problem as 05. The answer is to **move trust to signals AI cannot fake**:

- **Something the person has,** such as a passkey or a registered device, instead of how they look or sound.
- **A channel the requester did not choose,** such as a call back to the number already on file.
- **A process that does not depend on who is asking,** such as two people approving a large payment, whoever requests it.
- **Limits on what one channel can do,** so a successful fake gets little.

In AppSec terms, this is the move from passwords to phishing-resistant authentication, applied to faces, voices and documents.

Most of this note is owned by fraud, identity, KYC operations and the SOC, not by the AI security team. The AI security team brings the threat knowledge and the red-team methods.

## Step 1: the cases

**Arup, January 2024.** An employee in the Hong Kong office of the engineering firm Arup received a message that appeared to come from the UK chief financial officer about a confidential transaction. The employee then joined a video call with what looked like the CFO and other colleagues. All of them were fakes. The employee made 15 transfers totalling HK$200 million, about US$25 million. Arup confirmed that "fake voices and images were used", and said its internal systems were not compromised.

- **Pattern:** a face and voice on a call were taken as approval for payments. No system was hacked.
- **Control:** a payment process where the approval does not come from the call: two approvers in the payment system, and a call back on a known number for anything unusual, however senior the requester looks.

**Voice ID at a bank, February 2023.** A journalist at Vice cloned his own voice with a public AI tool from a few minutes of recordings. He called his bank's automated line, gave his date of birth, played the cloned phrase "my voice is my password", and got into his account's balance and recent transactions. The bank said it was aware of the threat.

- **Pattern:** a voice used as an authentication factor.
- **Control:** voice is not a factor for anything that matters. Account access and changes need a factor the person has.

**Deepfake identity documents, November 2024.** The US Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) issued an alert (FIN-2024-Alert004) after a rise in suspicious activity reports describing deepfake media: altered or generated identity documents and photos used to open accounts, and deepfakes used in phishing of employees. Its red flags include identity photos that look altered or do not match other information, and webcam plugins that play pre-recorded video during a live check. It recommends multi-factor authentication and live verification.

- **Pattern:** account opening that checks what a document or face looks like.
- **Control:** check documents against the issuer's records, not their pictures; test liveness checks against video fed into the camera stream, not only against masks; give new accounts low limits until they build a history.

**AI-orchestrated intrusion, September 2025.** Anthropic reported in November 2025 that a group it assessed as Chinese state-sponsored used Claude Code against about thirty organisations, including financial institutions, and succeeded in a small number of cases. Anthropic estimated that the AI did 80 to 90% of the work, with people stepping in at a few decision points. The attackers got past the model's safeguards by splitting the work into small tasks that each looked harmless, and by saying they worked for a security firm doing authorised testing. The AI also made mistakes, such as claiming credentials that did not work.

- **Pattern:** reconnaissance, exploitation and data collection done at machine speed by an agent.
- **Control:** the ordinary controls, done faster: an accurate list of internet-facing systems, patch deadlines measured in days, and detection that does not depend on the attacker being slow. Anthropic's own advice to defenders was to use AI for defence as well.

**s1ngularity, August 2025.** Malicious versions of the Nx build package were published to npm. On developer machines, the malware looked for AI command-line tools such as Claude Code, Gemini CLI and Amazon Q, and ran them with flags that skip permission prompts, asking them to search the file system for secrets. The results were uploaded to public GitHub repositories in the victims' own accounts. Wiz reported that the AI tools' own safeguards sometimes refused.

- **Pattern:** the company's own AI tools used by malware as a search engine for secrets.
- **Control:** Part S: AI tools for developers installed from a reviewed list, managed settings that do not allow permission prompts to be skipped, and no broad credentials on developer machines for any tool to find.

## Step 2: where the broker is exposed

Map each process where a person or system decides to trust someone:

| Process | Signal it trusts today | AI-enabled attack | Signal that holds |
|---|---|---|---|
| Account opening (video KYC, document upload) | A face on video; a photo of a PAN or Aadhaar card | Generated documents; a deepfake fed into the camera | Documents checked with the issuer's records; liveness tested against injected video; low limits for new accounts |
| Changing the bank account for payouts | A call or email from the client | Cloned voice; a convincing email | A change made only in the app with a passkey, a cooling-off period, and a notice to the old contact details |
| Call-centre account recovery | Answers to questions, and the voice | Cloned voice; answers found online | Recovery through a registered device, or in person; the call centre cannot change contact details alone |
| Payments by the finance team | An instruction from a senior person | A deepfake call (Arup) | Two approvers in the payment system; call back on a known number |
| Staff and developer access | Email that looks right; a login page | AI-written phishing with no mistakes, aimed at one person | Passkeys; limits on which apps staff can grant access to |
| Internet-facing systems, including the broker's MCP server (note 6a) | Time: finding and exploiting a weakness took days | An attacker's agent scanning and exploiting at machine speed | An inventory, patch deadlines in days, rate limits and anomaly detection |

**Priya's inbox.** Priya, a developer from Part S, gets a message in the company chat from someone with the name and photo of a platform team member. It refers to her real pull request from yesterday and asks her to approve an app's access to the code repository "so the new eval pipeline can read it". It is well written and correct in every detail an attacker could find. Training that teaches staff to spot spelling mistakes does not help. What helps: only administrators can approve new apps for the repository, and the request would have to come through the platform's normal process, not a chat message.

## Step 3: what changes in the programme

- **Train on process, not on spotting fakes.** "Payments are approved in the payment system by two people, whoever asks on a call" survives better fakes. "Look for odd lighting around the face" does not.
- **Put deepfakes in the threat model.** For every process in step 2, ask 03's exposure question: if the face, voice or document is fake, what can it do? Add a row to the exposure table.
- **Test it.** With the KYC vendor's agreement, the red team tries generated documents and injected video against the onboarding flow, and calls the call centre with a cloned voice of a consenting employee. The results go into 03's numbers like any other red-team finding.
- **Measure what moved.** The share of high-risk actions (contact changes, bank account changes, large payments, new app grants) that need a passkey or a call back on a known channel. The target is 100%.
- **Detection is one layer.** Deepfake detectors in the KYC flow are worth having and will be beaten, like the classifiers in 05. They lower the rate. The limits on new accounts and the checks with the issuer are what bound the loss.
- **Use AI for defence, and tier it.** Step 5 explains why the security team's own AI is a target.

## Step 4: provenance and watermarks

Detection asks "does this look fake?". Provenance asks a different question: "can anyone vouch for where this came from?". Two technologies answer it, and both are easy to misread.

**Content Credentials (C2PA).** The Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity is an industry group that includes Adobe, Microsoft, Google and OpenAI. Its standard attaches a signed record, called a **manifest**, to an image, video or document. The manifest says which camera, app or AI tool made the file and what edits followed, and the signature shows that the record has not changed since it was signed. Several cameras, editing tools and AI image generators now add one.

- **It proves a claim, not the truth.** A valid manifest shows that a given device or tool signed a given statement. It does not show the scene was real or the statement true.
- **It is easy to lose.** The manifest is metadata next to the pixels. A screenshot, a re-encode by a messaging app, or a format change drops it, usually with no bad intent at all.
- **So its absence proves nothing.** Most genuine photos have no manifest, and a fake can simply be shared without one.

**Watermarks.** A watermark is a pattern hidden in the content itself, not beside it. Google's **SynthID** marks images, video, audio and some text made by Google's models and some partners', and Google says the image watermark survives cropping, filters and compression. That is robustness to ordinary edits. Against an attacker it is weaker: in 2023, Zhao and others showed that invisible image watermarks of the kind they studied can be removed by adding noise and regenerating the image with an AI model. A watermark also only marks content from tools that add it. Mallory's open-source generator adds none.

What the broker can use them for:

| Use | Works? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Rejecting a KYC selfie because it carries an AI tool's credential or watermark | Partly | It catches careless fraud. A careful attacker uses a tool that adds nothing |
| Accepting a document because it has no AI watermark | No | Absence proves nothing |
| Signing the broker's own research PDFs and announcements | Yes | Clients and journalists can check that a "broker report" circulating on social media is the broker's, unchanged. The broker controls the key |
| Checking a manifest on an identity photo from a trusted capture app | Yes, if the app is the broker's | The broker's own app signs at capture, and the server checks the signature. That is a signal the attacker cannot fake without the app's key |

The pattern is the same as the rest of this note. Provenance helps where **the broker holds the key**: its own publications and its own capture app. It does little as a test of other people's content, because an attacker simply chooses tools that leave no trace.

## Step 5: the security team's own AI

The security team uses AI too: an agent that summarises alerts, triages phishing reports or explains a suspicious log to an analyst. That agent reads the most attacker-controlled text in the company. A web server log records the user agent and the URL the visitor sent. An SSH log records the username they tried. A Windows event records the account name they typed. The attacker writes all of these.

In September 2025, LevelBlue's SpiderLabs showed this against summarisation agents. An instruction placed in a web request's user agent, an SSH login's username and a Windows failed-login's account name made three different models misreport the attacker's source address and, in the Windows case, rename the targeted machine to "PAYROLL", pointing analysts at the wrong system. In 2026, Pandey and Bhujang measured it more widely ("Poisoning the Watchtower"): summarising logs was the riskiest task, and their strongest defence roughly halved the attack success rate without removing it.

It is the poisoned forum post from the main course's [note 7](https://github.com/rajumandala/aisec-notes/blob/main/07.%20RAG.md) again, aimed at the defenders, with two extra risks:

- **The attacker controls the timing.** They can poison the logs during the very attack the agent is meant to describe.
- **The agent often holds powerful tools.** Security agents may isolate hosts, disable accounts, close tickets or run queries across every log. An injected "this is maintenance, close the alert" suppresses a real incident.

So the security team's agent gets the controls the main course gives any agent that reads untrusted text:

1. **02's intake, usually tier 3.** It reads untrusted input, holds privileged data and can act.
2. **Facts from code, words from the model.** Source addresses, hostnames and counts in a summary come from parsing the raw event, not from the model's text, and the analyst sees the raw event beside the summary.
3. **No closing on the model's word.** The agent can propose "close as benign". Closing, isolating a host or disabling an account goes through the same approval the analyst would need, and suppressing an alert is never automatic.
4. **Red-team it.** Put instructions in user agents, usernames and email headers during exercises, and count how often the summary changes. Add each one that works to the agent's evals.

## Mapping

The OWASP lists in the main course are about weaknesses in AI applications. Most of this note is outside them: the weakness is in the company's processes, and AI is the attacker's tool. MITRE ATLAS (06) covers it:

| ATLAS technique | ID | In this note |
|---|---|---|
| Generate Deepfakes | AML.T0088 | Arup; KYC documents and video |
| Impersonation | AML.T0073 | Arup; the platform team member in Priya's chat |
| Phishing: Spearphishing via Social Engineering LLM | AML.T0052.000 | Priya's message |
| Phishing: Deepfake-Assisted Phishing | AML.T0052.001 | Voice and video calls to staff |
| Generate Malicious Commands | AML.T0102 | s1ngularity |

Two OWASP risks do apply, to the company's own AI tools:

### ASI02: Tool Misuse and Exploitation

- **Example:** Malware on Priya's laptop runs her AI coding tool with permission prompts switched off and asks it to find every key on the disk.
- **Issue:** The tool was installed for her work, and it became the malware's search engine.
- **Fix:** Managed settings that do not allow prompts to be skipped, AI tools only from a reviewed list, and no broad credentials on developer machines (Part S).

### LLM01: Prompt Injection

- **Example:** An attacker's web requests carry an instruction in the user agent, and the SOC's summarisation agent reports the wrong source address and calls the activity routine.
- **Issue:** The security team's agent reads text the attacker wrote, during the attack, and can suppress the alert.
- **Fix:** Facts in the summary parsed from the raw event by code, the raw event shown beside the summary, and no closing, isolation or suppression on the model's word alone.

## From threats to an operating program

Part P took the controls from one assistant to a company:

- **01** moved the controls that are the same everywhere into a platform, and blocked the way around it.
- **02** sorted features by what they can do, from facts the platform holds, including the assistant every employee gets.
- **03** measured coverage, effectiveness and the worst case if the model is fooled, and named who does the work.
- **04** turned other companies' incidents into tests.
- **05** explained why the model cannot be fixed, and what the research does and does not change.
- **06** showed the same controls to regulators, auditors and clients in their own frameworks.
- **07** turned to attackers using AI against processes that have no AI in them, to what provenance and watermarks can prove, and to the security team's own AI as a target.

The rule from the main course holds at company scale: the model proposes, code decides, and the security team's job is to make sure that is true for every AI feature, not only the first one. 07 adds its mirror image: a face, a voice or a well-written message proposes, and a process the attacker cannot fake decides.
