# P6. Standards Map

P5 ended with a security team that can explain its controls and their limits. Then the questionnaires arrive. The board's audit committee asks about the NIST AI RMF. A large client wants ISO/IEC 42001. The EU subsidiary asks about the EU AI Act. The red team's report uses MITRE ATLAS. Each asks for evidence in a different shape, and answering each from scratch would take more time than running the controls.

## The idea

Keep **one list of controls**, with an owner and an evidence source for each, and map that list to every framework. A framework then becomes a view of controls the company already runs, not a new project.

The frameworks do different jobs, so they ask different questions:

| Framework | What it is | What it asks | Who asks |
|---|---|---|---|
| NIST AI RMF 1.0 (2023), with the Generative AI Profile, NIST AI 600-1 (2024) | Voluntary guidance, organised as four functions: Govern, Map, Measure, Manage | Do you know your AI risks, and do you manage them on purpose? | Boards, US clients, internal audit |
| ISO/IEC 42001:2023 | A certifiable management system for AI, like ISO/IEC 27001 for information security | Is there a policy, roles, a risk process, controls chosen with reasons, internal audit and management review? | Enterprise clients, certification auditors |
| EU AI Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 | Law, with duties set by the system's risk class and the company's role | Which class is each system in, are you its provider or deployer, and have you met that class's duties? | Regulators, EU entities and clients |
| MITRE ATLAS | A knowledge base of real attack techniques against AI systems, in the style of MITRE ATT&CK | Which techniques can reach you, and which do you detect or block? | Red teams, threat intelligence, the SOC |
| OWASP Top 10 lists (LLM, Agentic, MCP) | Lists of the most important risks for developers | Has each risk been considered in each feature? | Developers, AppSec, the main course |
| India: SEBI, DPDP Act, CERT-In | Regulation for a broker in India | Covered in note 15 and P4 | SEBI, auditors, CERT-In |

In AppSec terms, this is the same as keeping one security control set and mapping it to ISO 27001, SOC 2 and PCI DSS, instead of running three programmes.

This note is a map, not legal advice. The EU AI Act dates below are as of October 2026, and the law has already changed once since it was passed.

## Step 1: what each framework really wants

**NIST AI RMF.** Four functions. *Govern*: policy, roles, accountability and culture. *Map*: know each system's context, purpose and risks. *Measure*: test and track them. *Manage*: act on them, and respond when things go wrong. The Generative AI Profile adds twelve risks that generative AI creates or makes worse. Four of them are the course's subject: information security, data privacy, confabulation (models inventing facts), and value chain and component integration (third-party models, data and tools). It is voluntary, and it asks for outcomes, not specific controls.

**ISO/IEC 42001.** The body of the standard asks for a management system: scope, an AI policy, roles, a risk assessment, an AI system impact assessment, objectives, internal audits and management reviews. Annex A gives reference controls in nine groups, A.2 to A.10: policies, internal organisation, resources, impact assessment, the system life cycle, data, information for interested parties, use of AI systems, and third-party and customer relationships. The company chooses which apply and says why in a **Statement of Applicability**. A certificate says the management system works. It does not say any model is secure.

**EU AI Act.** Duties depend on the risk class and on whether the company is the **provider** (it builds the system or puts its name on it) or the **deployer** (it uses one). The parts relevant to the broker:

- Prohibited practices have applied since February 2025. Duties for providers of general-purpose AI models have applied since August 2025. Those fall mostly on the model providers, not the broker.
- Transparency duties under Article 50 apply from August 2026: people must be told when they are dealing with an AI system, unless it is obvious. An EU client chatting with the order assistant is told so.
- High-risk systems are listed in Annex III. Credit scoring of individuals is on it; order assistants and research summaries generally are not. The Digital Omnibus (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, in force since 27 July 2026) moved the start of Annex III high-risk duties from August 2026 to 2 December 2027.
- Article 15 asks high-risk systems for accuracy, robustness and cybersecurity, and names AI-specific attacks: data poisoning, model poisoning, adversarial examples and confidentiality attacks. It does not say how. The course is one answer to "how".

The Act applies when a system is placed on the EU market or its output is used in the EU. An Indian broker with no EU clients may be outside it, but its clients and vendors may not be, and their questionnaires will ask anyway.

**MITRE ATLAS.** Tactics and techniques, each with an ID, taken from real attacks and research. Recent versions add many techniques for agents. Examples, from ATLAS data version 5.6.0:

The mapping columns are filled from the control outwards. Start from what the company does, then look up which framework line it answers. Starting from the framework produces controls written to match its wording that nobody runs.

## Step 3: evidence once

Most evidence already exists, because the platform produces it (P1 to P3):

- **Inventory:** the gateway key list and the agent registry answer "what AI systems do you have?" for every framework.
- **Risk assessment:** the intake and tier (P2), the threat model for tier 3 (note 14) and the exposure table (P3) answer NIST *Map*, ISO's risk and impact assessment, and the EU AI Act's classification.
- **Testing:** eval results and red-team reports (notes 5, 10, P3) answer NIST *Measure*, ISO A.6 and EU Article 15. Tagging each red-team case with its ATLAS ID gives the coverage table for free.
- **Response:** drill records and incident reports (P3, P4) answer NIST *Manage* and ISO's corrective actions.
- **Third parties:** the MCP registry, vendor intake and the provider settings at the gateway (P1, P2) answer ISO A.10 and the vendor sections of every questionnaire.

Store each piece once, with the date it was produced, and link the framework views to it. When a client sends a 200-question questionnaire, most answers are a link.

## Step 4: traps

- **Compliance is not security.** Every framework here can be satisfied by a company whose order assistant has the lethal trifecta. NIST asks that risks are managed, ISO asks that a process exists, and the EU AI Act asks for an "appropriate level" of cybersecurity. None says "no images in replies". The controls come from the threat, and the frameworks check they are organised.
- **Mapping everything to everything.** A control mapped to fifteen framework lines usually answers none of them well. Map to the lines it really meets.
- **A certificate's scope.** A vendor's ISO/IEC 42001 or 27001 certificate covers the vendor's management system, for the scope written on it. It does not cover how the broker uses the vendor's model or tools (P2's vendor intake still applies).
- **Frameworks lag attacks.** ATLAS added its agent techniques after attacks on agents were public, and the OWASP lists are revised every year or two. P4's case studies and P5's research reach the team before the frameworks do.
- **Dates move.** The EU AI Act's high-risk dates changed in 2026. Keep the dates in one place, with the source, and check them before a decision depends on them.

## OWASP risks in this note

The codes come from the [OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications](https://github.com/rajumandala/aisec-notes/blob/main/A1.%20OWASP%20Top%2010%20for%20LLM%20Applications.md) and the [OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications](https://github.com/rajumandala/aisec-notes/blob/main/A2.%20OWASP%20Top%2010%20for%20Agentic%20Applications.md), both 2026 editions. Nothing in this note has been run, so each example shows what would happen.

### LLM01: Prompt Injection

- **Example:** The broker passes an ISO/IEC 42001 audit. The support-drafting tool still renders links from retrieved tickets.
- **Issue:** The audit checked that a risk process existed and that the tool had been assessed. It did not check the assessment was right.
- **Fix:** Controls come from the threat model and P4's cases. The certificate is evidence that they are managed, not that they work.

### LLM02: Sensitive Information Disclosure

- **Example:** A client questionnaire asks where client data goes. The answer is written from memory and leaves out the tracing vendor.
- **Issue:** Evidence written for the questionnaire, not taken from the system.
- **Fix:** C-02 and C-12: data flows come from the gateway config and the trace schema, and the answer links to them.

### ASI04: Agentic Supply Chain Vulnerabilities

- **Example:** An MCP server vendor sends an ISO/IEC 27001 certificate, and procurement marks the review done.
- **Issue:** The certificate does not cover the tool descriptions the server publishes, or changes to them.
- **Fix:** The certificate is one input to vendor intake (P2). The server is still pinned and registered (C-07).

## End of Part P

Part P took the controls from one assistant to a company:

- **P1** moved the controls that are the same everywhere into a platform, and blocked the way around it.
- **P2** sorted features by what they can do, from facts the platform holds.
- **P3** measured coverage, effectiveness and the worst case if the model is fooled.
- **P4** turned other companies' incidents into tests.
- **P5** explained why the model cannot be fixed, and what the research does and does not change.
- **P6** showed the same controls to regulators, auditors and clients in their own frameworks.

The rule from the main course holds at company scale: the model proposes, code decides, and the security team's job is to make sure that is true for every AI feature, not only the first one.
