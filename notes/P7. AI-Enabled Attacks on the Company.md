# P7. AI-Enabled Attacks on the Company

P6 ended with the company's AI features mapped to every framework that asks about them. All of Part P so far has been about the AI the company builds or buys. Attackers use AI too, and most of what they attack has no AI in it: the account opening process, the call centre, the payments desk, the staff inbox and the developers' laptops.

## The idea

Many security checks rest on signals that used to be hard to fake: a face on a video call, a voice on the phone, a photo of an identity document, a well-written email from a colleague, the time it takes to find and exploit a weak server. Generative AI makes each of these cheaper to fake or faster to do. The attacks are not new. Their cost has fallen.

So the answer is not mainly to detect fakes. Detection is a classifier against an attacker who moves second, the same problem as P5. The answer is to **move trust to signals AI cannot fake**:

- **Something the person has,** such as a passkey or a registered device, instead of how they look or sound.
- **A channel the requester did not choose,** such as a call back to the number already on file.
- **A process that does not depend on who is asking,** such as two people approving a large payment, whoever requests it.
- **Limits on what one channel can do,** so a successful fake gets little.

In AppSec terms, this is the move from passwords to phishing-resistant authentication, applied to faces, voices and documents.

Most of this note is owned by fraud, identity, KYC operations and the SOC, not by the AI security team. The AI security team brings the threat knowledge and the red-team methods. This note is design and process, and nothing in it has been run.

## Step 1: the cases

**Arup, January 2024.** An employee in the Hong Kong office of the engineering firm Arup received a message that appeared to come from the UK chief financial officer about a confidential transaction. The employee then joined a video call with what looked like the CFO and other colleagues. All of them were fakes. The employee made 15 transfers totalling HK$200 million, about US$25 million. Arup confirmed that "fake voices and images were used", and said its internal systems were not compromised.

- **Pattern:** a face and voice on a call were taken as approval for payments. No system was hacked.
- **Control:** a payment process where the approval does not come from the call: two approvers in the payment system, and a call back on a known number for anything unusual, however senior the requester looks.

**Voice ID at a bank, February 2023.** A journalist at Vice cloned his own voice with a public AI tool from a few minutes of recordings. He called his bank's automated line, gave his date of birth, played the cloned phrase "my voice is my password", and got into his account's balance and recent transactions. The bank said it was aware of the threat.

- **Pattern:** a voice used as an authentication factor.
- **Control:** voice is not a factor for anything that matters. Account access and changes need a factor the person has.

**Deepfake identity documents, November 2024.** The US Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) issued an alert (FIN-2024-Alert004) after a rise in suspicious activity reports describing deepfake media: altered or generated identity documents and photos used to open accounts, and deepfakes used in phishing of employees. Its red flags include identity photos that look altered or do not match other information, and webcam plugins that play pre-recorded video during a live check. It recommends multi-factor authentication and live verification.

- **Pattern:** account opening that checks what a document or face looks like.
- **Control:** check documents against the issuer's records, not their pictures; test liveness checks against video fed into the camera stream, not only against masks; give new accounts low limits until they build a history.

**AI-orchestrated intrusion, September 2025.** Anthropic reported in November 2025 that a group it assessed as Chinese state-sponsored used Claude Code against about thirty organisations, including financial institutions, and succeeded in a small number of cases. Anthropic estimated that the AI did 80 to 90% of the work, with people stepping in at a few decision points. The attackers got past the model's safeguards by splitting the work into small tasks that each looked harmless, and by saying they worked for a security firm doing authorised testing. The AI also made mistakes, such as claiming credentials that did not work.

- **Pattern:** reconnaissance, exploitation and data collection done at machine speed by an agent.
- **Control:** the ordinary controls, done faster: an accurate list of internet-facing systems, patch deadlines measured in days, and detection that does not depend on the attacker being slow. Anthropic's own advice to defenders was to use AI for defence as well.

**s1ngularity, August 2025.** Malicious versions of the Nx build package were published to npm. On developer machines, the malware looked for AI command-line tools such as Claude Code, Gemini CLI and Amazon Q, and ran them with flags that skip permission prompts, asking them to search the file system for secrets. The results were uploaded to public GitHub repositories in the victims' own accounts. Wiz reported that the AI tools' own safeguards sometimes refused.

- **Pattern:** the company's own AI tools used by malware as a search engine for secrets.
- **Control:** Part S: AI tools for developers installed from a reviewed list, managed settings that do not allow permission prompts to be skipped, and no broad credentials on developer machines for any tool to find.

## Step 2: where the broker is exposed

Map each process where a person or system decides to trust someone:

| Process | Signal it trusts today | AI-enabled attack | Signal that holds |
|---|---|---|---|
| Account opening (video KYC, document upload) | A face on video; a photo of a PAN or Aadhaar card | Generated documents; a deepfake fed into the camera | Documents checked with the issuer's records; liveness tested against injected video; low limits for new accounts |
| Changing the bank account for payouts | A call or email from the client | Cloned voice; a convincing email | A change made only in the app with a passkey, a cooling-off period, and a notice to the old contact details |
| Call-centre account recovery | Answers to questions, and the voice | Cloned voice; answers found online | Recovery through a registered device, or in person; the call centre cannot change contact details alone |
| Payments by the finance team | An instruction from a senior person | A deepfake call (Arup) | Two approvers in the payment system; call back on a known number |
| Staff and developer access | Email that looks right; a login page | AI-written phishing with no mistakes, aimed at one person | Passkeys; limits on which apps staff can grant access to |
| Internet-facing systems, including the broker's MCP server (note 6a) | Time: finding and exploiting a weakness took days | An attacker's agent scanning and exploiting at machine speed | An inventory, patch deadlines in days, rate limits and anomaly detection |

**Priya's inbox.** Priya, a developer from Part S, gets a message in the company chat from someone with the name and photo of a platform team member. It refers to her real pull request from yesterday and asks her to approve an app's access to the code repository "so the new eval pipeline can read it". It is well written and correct in every detail an attacker could find. Training that teaches staff to spot spelling mistakes does not help. What helps: only administrators can approve new apps for the repository, and the request would have to come through the platform's normal process, not a chat message.

## Step 3: what changes in the programme

- **Train on process, not on spotting fakes.** "Payments are approved in the payment system by two people, whoever asks on a call" survives better fakes. "Look for odd lighting around the face" does not.
- **Put deepfakes in the threat model.** For every process in step 2, ask P3's exposure question: if the face, voice or document is fake, what can it do? Add a row to the exposure table.
- **Test it.** With the KYC vendor's agreement, the red team tries generated documents and injected video against the onboarding flow, and calls the call centre with a cloned voice of a consenting employee. The results go into P3's numbers like any other red-team finding.
- **Measure what moved.** The share of high-risk actions (contact changes, bank account changes, large payments, new app grants) that need a passkey or a call back on a known channel. The target is 100%.
- **Detection is one layer.** Deepfake detectors in the KYC flow are worth having and will be beaten, like the classifiers in P5. They lower the rate. The limits on new accounts and the checks with the issuer are what bound the loss.
- **Use AI for defence, and tier it.** An agent that triages alerts or reviews code for the security team reads untrusted text (alerts, logs, attackers' payloads) and holds privileged access. It goes through P2's intake like any other feature, and it is usually tier 3.

## Mapping

The OWASP lists in the main course are about weaknesses in AI applications. Most of this note is outside them: the weakness is in the company's processes, and AI is the attacker's tool. MITRE ATLAS (P6) covers it:

| ATLAS technique | ID | In this note |
|---|---|---|
| Generate Deepfakes | AML.T0088 | Arup; KYC documents and video |
| Impersonation | AML.T0073 | Arup; the platform team member in Priya's chat |
| Phishing: Spearphishing via Social Engineering LLM | AML.T0052.000 | Priya's message |
| Phishing: Deepfake-Assisted Phishing | AML.T0052.001 | Voice and video calls to staff |
| Generate Malicious Commands | AML.T0102 | s1ngularity |

One OWASP risk does apply, to the company's own AI tools:

### ASI02: Tool Misuse and Exploitation

- **Example:** Malware on Priya's laptop runs her AI coding tool with permission prompts switched off and asks it to find every key on the disk.
- **Issue:** The tool was installed for her work, and it became the malware's search engine.
- **Fix:** Managed settings that do not allow prompts to be skipped, AI tools only from a reviewed list, and no broad credentials on developer machines (Part S).

## End of Part P

Part P took the controls from one assistant to a company:

- **P1** moved the controls that are the same everywhere into a platform, and blocked the way around it.
- **P2** sorted features by what they can do, from facts the platform holds, including the assistant every employee gets.
- **P3** measured coverage, effectiveness and the worst case if the model is fooled, and named who does the work.
- **P4** turned other companies' incidents into tests.
- **P5** explained why the model cannot be fixed, and what the research does and does not change.
- **P6** showed the same controls to regulators, auditors and clients in their own frameworks.
- **P7** turned to attackers using AI against processes that have no AI in them.

The rule from the main course holds at company scale: the model proposes, code decides, and the security team's job is to make sure that is true for every AI feature, not only the first one. P7 adds its mirror image: a face, a voice or a well-written message proposes, and a process the attacker cannot fake decides.
